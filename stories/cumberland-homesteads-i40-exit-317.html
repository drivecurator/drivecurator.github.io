Cumberland Homesteads and the Homesteads Tower, near I-40 exit 317 in Crossville

From I-40 at Crossville, Exit 317 puts you on U.S. 127. Head south about four miles from town and you reach a stone tower at the junction with Highway 68. It's the center of one of the most interesting New Deal experiments in the country.

In the Depression, many miners and timber workers on the Cumberland Plateau had been out of work for years. Starting in 1934, the federal government began building a planned farm community here. Those same unemployed men, the future homesteaders, cleared the land and built the houses. Pennsylvania architect William Macy Stanton, who had worked on house designs for the town of Norris, laid out the roads and designed the buildings.

The houses are what you notice from the car. Most are a story and a half, with walls of local Crab Orchard sandstone and gable roofs. The lumber came from the land around them, and the community blacksmith made the door hinges. Every house got plumbing and wiring, and TVA power arrived in December 1937. Not everyone approved. Tennessee Senator Kenneth McKellar complained that relief workers were living in stone mansions better than his own house.

Planners hoped for 351 farms, but only 251 were finished when the project was celebrated as complete on July 28, 1938. It was the largest community built by the Division of Subsistence Homesteads. The farms proved too small and the soil too poor, and many men went off to work in Detroit, Akron, and at TVA dams, sending money home.

The octagonal tower, built in 1937 and 1938, held the project's offices and a water tank. It opened as a museum in 1984. Climb its 97 steps and you can see most of the original farmsteads, still looking much as they did. The district is on the National Register of Historic Places.
